Rowling Chapter 1 Chapter Notes See the end of the chapter for notes Thomas Potter – only his father called him “Tom” to his face – was not having a good day. The mission itself went well, of course. Tom had spent days begging his father on hands and knees to be allowed to tag along, and against better judgement his father conceded. On conditions: Tom had to behave, and Tom had to obey. He had wondered if his father had been drunk that evening, because otherwise Tom could not fathom why the man had gone along with something so obviously doomed to fail. Still, he had nodded with the sincerest fake smile he could muster. His father had not bought it. Tom had seen that in his eyes. But his father had remained silent. His father was all about making free choices and living with the consequences. He wouldn’t punish Tom on precaution and not-so-baseless presumption alone. Naturally, he would keep an extra sharp eye on Tom, waiting for him to step out of line so he could pounce like a bloodhound. Tom shivered at the thought. His father was…well-mannered, for lack of a better word, and loving most of the time. Tom definitely didn’t like his father mad. Angry at Tom, that was. His father could be angry at his followers all he liked. Or James. James had hexed Tom’s cloak to billow heroically at random intervals last week. It took Tom two hours to get through that spell work. It had been his favorite cloak. Yeah, fuck James. Anyway, it was hilarious to see battle-hardened men and women shift nervously around like children caught red-handed, all their discipline useless under Father’s gaze of heartfelt disappointment. Had shame been lethal, that look would have killed thousands, which was exactly why Tom went through great lengths to minimize the risk of getting caught whenever he did something he shouldn’t – because, let’s face it, he was his father’s son. Potters didn’t behave and Potters would never simply obey . The leader of the group Tom had been assigned to, turned out to be a sympathizer. Or an apologist. Or simply a traitor. Tom did not particularly care for the distinction. The man had projected so hard Tom felt second-hand embarrassment. For reasons Tom didn't quite catch and didn't give a fuck about, the man had been about to betray and kill half the group to sell one of their most valuable assets to the enemy: namely, Tom. Tom’s life held enormous political weight, and he did not fancy being kidnapped. Again. The first time had been fun, mostly because he had gotten to see his father at full power. But after the second and third and fourth time, kidnapping had really lost its appeal. So, with a clear conscience, Tom beheaded the man with a clean, overpowered Diffindo before he could betray them – his father wouldn’t have blamed Tom for that. His father would take the completion of the mission over whatever minor gain interrogating the traitor would have brought. No, the detail that ruined Tom’s day came afterwards: When he told the team he saved their asses, they did not believe him. People usually knew Tom as either a manipulative little shit or a charming young man, depending on who you asked. This team seemed to have settled somewhere in between, which was frankly inconvenient. One of them even insisted there must have been a good reason for the man’s almost-betrayal. There were no good reasons for betrayal. That she had been sleeping with him didn't somehow change that fact magically. She did not take that comment well. Tom did not fucking care. In the end, they either could not or would not or did not want to believe him. Denial, most likely. Unfortunately, the mission was time-sensitive and important enough that they could not simply stop, turn around, and argue about whether Tom had murdered their leader for shits and giggles or had indeed saved all their lives. So, they took Tom prisoner. Which meant he surrendered his wand and walked in front of the group. Tom complied. None of them had any illusions about Tom being harmless without a wand. Tom was just as dangerous unarmed as most people were fully equipped. But if that little bit of control made them feel better, Tom was happy to oblige. Obviously, his happy smile was fake. If Tom had wanted to kill them, he would have killed them. The only reason he did not was their loyalty to his father and their usefulness to the cause. As he had said, the mission itself went well. They reached the objective, did what they had come to do, and made it out with only the average amount of blood, panic, and incompetence. It was afterwards that people started getting spell–happy. He felt the AK before it even left Miss Ensen's wand. Tom wandlessly conjured a thick stone slab to absorb the spell. The woman’s Killing Curse didn't even leave a scorch mark on it. It must have been her first time casting it. It did not matter. It was an AK. It probably would have given Tom a nosebleed at best, but that was not the point. The point was that it triggered his ward. Specifically, the ward that informed his father whenever an AK was used in Tom’s proximity. A ward Tom – till now – had not managed to get rid of. His father was behind Tom a heartbeat later. Tom could tell from the way the team stared past him: wide-eyed, awed and terrified, like they wanted to worship the ground his father stood on but were too afraid to move. Tom did not bother turning around. His father stopped at his side, and Tom tipped his face up, because even after his recent growth spurt Tom still needed to look up at him. He met his father’s killing-curse green eyes, burning with cold fury. One would think age would mellow him out eventually. Apparently not. “Show me,” his father growled. Tom obediently lowered his Occlumency barriers, because one did not disobey a man with the magical power of a natural disaster. Even if he was your father. His father did not rip into his mind. If Tom had not known him as well as he did, he might have been surprised. But as it was, Tom knew his father very well. Tom guided him through the relevant memories first: the traitor, the almost-betrayal, the teams’ disbelief, the forced surrender of his wand and finally the woman raising her wand and casting the Killing Curse at Tom’s back. His father followed each memory Tom offered up to him, patiently. Then, the pressure of his father’s magic vanished from Tom’s mind so completely that for one sharp second Tom felt disorientated. Tom blinked up at his father, raised his Occlumency barriers again and waited. His father had seen the memory of who cast the Killing Curse, the little detail his father was most anxious about, and Tom had not cast it. Not would not . Had not. The Killing Curse was the one spell on the very short list of things Tom was categorically forbidden from casting, and for once it was not one of his father's arbitrary rules about bedtimes, indoor dueling or “no parseltongue in polite company” because it made people uncomfortable . His father's presence made people uncomfortable . Tom's soul was – in the clinical words of Healer Moreau – structurally compromised . Unstable. Cracked in places souls were not supposed to crack. Casting an AK required a soul that could take the recoil. Tom's would splinter like cheap glass, probably. His father had drilled that into him with the kind of relentless, quiet intensity that made even Tom not want to test it. Much. When his father turned – slowly – Tom knew he had reached exactly two conclusions. One: the hunch that there might be a traitor had been correct and having Tom deal with it was one of the better outcomes. Two: one of his own people, one of the men and women who wore his sigil and had sworn their loyalty to him, had pointed a wand at his son and spoken the words Avada Kedavra . Tom almost felt sorry for Miss Ensen. “You disarmed him”, his father observed. His voice was calm. Screaming was for people who needed volume to be taken seriously. “You disarmed my son, marched him at wandpoint through hostile territory, and then” – a pause – “you tried to kill him.” Nobody answered. Nobody breathed . The team stood in a loose half-circle of pure, distilled horror, and Tom watched on with interest as several of them visibly recalculated every decision that had led them to this moment. Miss Ensen's wand hand was shaking. Her mouth opened. Nothing came out. Wise choice, honestly. There was no combination of words in the English language that could have improved her situation. His father's magic began to move. Not visibly – his father was too tired for theatrics – but Tom felt it the way one felt a storm front: a pressure change, a wrongness in the air, the animal certainty that something enormous had just noticed you. One of them, Ross, made a very small sound, like a whimper that had been strangled at birth. And that – that was the exact moment when fate decided Tom's day was not yet bad enough. The magic came from nowhere and everywhere at once. It ignored the team and went for his father like an arrow. Tom recognized it a half-second before it made contact, because he had read about this exact class of magic in books he had no business reading. A summoning. A calling . Someone, somewhere – some other world – had lit a beacon and demanded a Harry Potter . Tom would have laughed if he'd had the time. Some poor doomed sod in some poor doomed world, on their knees around a ritual circle, praying for someone, for Harry Potter , to come save them from whatever they need saving from. They had no idea what they were calling. The magic, gold light, touched his father – and slid off . His father was too heavy. Too anchored. Too much . Trying to summon him was like trying to lift a mountain with a shovel; the magic strained, screamed, found no purchase on a soul that outweighed its entire ritual array – and did what most half-failed magic did: it went for the next best thing. Tom felt the hook set behind his sternum, and the world tilted. “No.” His father moved faster than anything human had a right to move – and for the first time in Tom's entire life, his father wasn't fast enough. Tom's feet had already left the ground. The gold light wrapped around him like a fist. He caught a last, fractured glimpse of the scene: the team, frozen in place; Miss Ensen, on the ground, shaking; his father, hand outstretched, green eyes blazing with something Tom had never seen there and would have preferred to never have seen at all and hoped to never see again. His father did not scream. But his lips moved. As the world dissolved into gold, the last thing Thomas Potter heard was half of a promise spoken with the calm of a man stating the inevitable. “–will find you.” Tom believed him. The landing hurt. Not physically – the ritual deposited him with almost insulting gentleness – but magically. The world tasted wrong. The ambient magic was thinner, younger, and it pressed against Tom's senses like a room with the wrong ceiling height. He was on one knee on a cold floor, in a kitchen that had seen better centuries, in a house whose very bones were saturated with dark magic. There was the familiar smell of candle wax, dust, sweat and desperation. He was surrounded by roughly a dozen people pointing wands at him. Tom kept his attention on them as the runes of the circle beneath him were – still glowing faintly gold – burning themselves out one by one. Fighters, most of them, judging by their stances, wary and tired. Three, maybe four, truly worth taking seriously. Tom stayed exactly where he was and did not move. He had been on the receiving end of enough wands to know that sudden movements were for amateurs and people with death wishes, and while he and James arguably qualified for the second category, he wasn’t planning to die today. So instead, he studied the circle. Runes in a dialect he half recognized – old theory, heavy ritual magic. At least it seemed that there was no blood in the ink and chalk. Rituals of this class came with conditions. Moving before he understood them would also have been stupid, and Tom had many flaws, but situational unawareness was not one of them. He wasn’t James. "That," a voice came from somewhere to his left, "doesn't look like Harry Potter." Astute observation. Tom lifted his head to deliver something appropriately cutting – and stopped. Tom's brain stalled for a full second, which for him was an eternity. At the head of the table stood an old man in midnight-blue robes, long silver beard, half-moon spectacles. Tom knew that face. Not from life; from old photographs, from chocolate frog cards that had somehow stayed in one piece through war, from his father's rare, carefully worded stories that always ended a sentence too early. Albus Dumbledore. Who had been dead for longer than Tom had been alive. Right. Not just another world, then. Another time. Dumbledore was staring at him. Not the way the others stared – alarmed, confused, wands wavering. Dumbledore stared at him the way one stared at a ghost. All the color had left the old man's face, and his wand, Tom noted, was in his hand, held low, and it had not been there a heartbeat ago. "Tom.” The old man’s voice was barely a breath. Tom felt something cold slide down his spine. Exactly one person called him “Tom” that way, and this man was not him. "Have we met?" Tom asked. Polite. Pleasant. His charming fake smile plastered on his face, the one that made his brother roll his eyes in disgust when it worked. It seemed to have the opposite of the intended effect: half the room raised their wands higher, and Dumbledore's expression went from ghost-seeing to something older and harder that Tom couldn't read and didn't like. "That," Dumbledore replied quietly, "is what I intend to find out. Your name, my boy?" My boy? Tom considered lying. He was good at lying. It was his natural first instinct. But he was wandless, outnumbered, in a foreign world, standing in the residue of a ritual that had been strong enough to yank him across dimensions – and something in the old man's gaze told him that a lie would be recognized, catalogued, and held against him. "Thomas Potter." Silence. The good kind, from Tom's perspective – the kind where he could watch a dozen people's worldviews derail in real time. The name Potter went through the room like a shattered glass. Wands wavered. "Potter," someone repeated. "Are you related to Harry Potter?", asked a red–haired woman by the stove. She was definitely a Weasley and bore a remarkable resemblance to his grandmother – which meant, she was this world’s Molly Weasley. She seemed far too young, still tried but not tired enough. Like she hadn’t lost half her children yet. Her voice had caught somewhere between hope and dread. It made Tom feel uncomfortable. "He's my father." Voices, all at once: – what? –impossible. –did the ritual go wrong?; –told you, we shouldn’t have– –well, look at him, he has Lily’s eyes – Between the noise, Albus Dumbledore did not speak, did not move at all, which Tom found significantly more concerning than if he had, because the old man's eyes flicked from Tom's face – cataloguing it feature by feature – to the burnt-out summoning circle and back, and whatever conclusion he was building, the old man did not share it. And at the far end of the kitchen, half-hidden behind two adults, stood a boy. Fifteen, sixteen at most. Messy black hair. Round glasses. A thin scar on his forehead and eyes, green eyes, killing-curse green, that Tom saw every single day of his life – in the mirror and in the face of a man whose fury registered on weather charts. His father and the boy looked similar the way a curse looked like the hand that cast it – not at all, except that once you knew, knew what to look for, you could not unknow it. Harry Potter was staring at Tom as though he'd been slapped. Not confused, like the others. Recognizing. Which made no sense, because Tom had very definitely never met a teenaged version of his own father in another world and – judging by the white-knuckled grip the boy had on his wand – whatever this Harry Potter recognized in Tom, it was nothing good. Tom filed that away next to Tom? and Dumbledore's ghost-face, in the rapidly growing folder of things his father had some explaining to do about. “Well, Mr. Potter”, Dumbledore addressed him softly, raising a hand. The room fell silent again with a speed that spoke of long practice. “We apologize for the rough welcoming. Forgive an cautious old man in a difficult hour but are things I must ask. Please, humor me.” “In my experience, humoring old men rarely ends well.” Tom rose to his feet, slowly, hands visible and open. Nobody hexed him. Progress. “You summoned me. Which, incidentally, is considered abduction in most legal systems – including, I suspect, yours. So perhaps you will answer me first. What do you want?” “They definitely didn't want you .” The boy had pushed out from behind the adults, voice gone flat and bitter. “Harry, please.” Dumbledore did not raise his voice. The boy didn’t say anything further. Just crossed his arm, visibly unhappy. Kept out of things, by the looks of him, and hating it. Tom knew that feeling. “We were calling for help”, the old man continued. His eyes had never left Tom's face, and there it was again, that recognition that had no business being there, paired with that vaguely horrified look. “Another Harry Potter. One who had faced his Voldemort – there was a collective flinch – and won. It would seem our ritual was slightly miscalibrated.” A Harry Potter who faced his Voldemort and won. Several things slotted into place in Tom's mind with the smoothness of a well-oiled lock. Voldemort . History called him with sympathy a man who derailed from a good cause because he drove himself mad and needed to be put down. Tom doubted people where thinking of Voldemort like that during his reign. His father qualified on the winning bit, he did, no question, but there were technicalities that these people definitely would not enjoy learning about. And apparently, their magic had reached across worlds, grabbed for the largest matching thing it could find, failed to lift it – and thus, settled on Tom as consolation prize. Tom stared to feel underappreciated. "Your ritual was calibrated just fine”, Tom informed them. He was kind like that, giving up free information. “Father just shrugged it off. You built a fishing rod and cast it at a leviathan. Congratulations. You caught his son instead.” “Then we send you back,” growled a scarred man with a mad, whirling blue eye, “and try again.” An elderly witch by the table shook her head. She had the chalk dust of the ritual circle still on her sleeves and the expression of a woman rereading her own arithmancy in mounting horror. “We can't. The circle – the return clause is conditional . It was meant as a safeguard, so our guest couldn't simply leave before...” She trailed off. “Before what?” Tom inquired, politely. "Before the death of He-Who-Must-Not-Be-Named.” Tom closed his eyes and allowed himself, internally, in the privacy of his own extremely well-shielded mind, one long and heartfelt curse. So that was the condition he'd felt settle over him like a fine net on arrival. He could not simply go home. Not until this world's Dark Lord was dead. Kidnapped. Again. To another world – that part was new, but still. And it came with homework this time. “Voldemort”, Tom clarified. That earned another flinch. Fascinating. Tom felt no magic or taboo behind the name as he said it, what were these people flinching for? “I need to kill him to go home, correct?” Dumbledore was opening his mouth to answer when the air in the middle of the kitchen started to rip . The people around him scattered back with commendable speed. With the sound of a world being treated like cheap parchment, the air tore open and out of the wound stepped a man in an immaculate black travel cloak worth more than this entire house: blond hair going silver at the temples, pointed aristocratic features, wand already out and sweeping the room for threats in a single passing arc. And directly behind Uncle Draco, stumbling through the closing gap because he had obviously jumped in at the last second, wand in hand and a grin on his face: James. “TOMMY”, his brother figure bellowed, with delight entirely unsuited to the current situation. “You are alive!” Tom suppressed, with monumental effort, the urge to test the circle’s edges to slap the “ Tommy ” out of James. Tom should have had enough reach if James didn’t move. Half their host's wands snapped to the newcomers. The other half seemed to be malfunctioning, because his brother was the image of their late grandfather – feature for feature, down to the careless hair – like their late grandfather and his brother’s namesake James Potter who presumably was also dead in this world. Several of wizards and witches present had clearly known that James personally, too, judging by the colour draining out of their faces. “You were told to stay”, Uncle Draco noted, without looking at James, in the tone of a man postponing a murder for logistical reasons. Then he took in the room properly – the wands, the faces, the old man at the head of the table, stopping briefly on a gloomy middle-aged man with a large hooked nose (the only thing Tom had noticed about him was how little there was to notice) – and Tom saw his uncle's expression cycle through recognition, disbelief and did something it almost never do: falter. Tom savoured the rare sight of his unflappable uncle flap. It was a rare pleasure. “Technically”, James argued, with the doomed courage of the chronically unwise, “I was told not to follow Dad . I followed you, Uncle Draco. That's–" "That's a Malfoy ," someone hissed from the crowd – one of the Weasleys, of which there appeared to be a set in this dimension, too. “That's definitely Draco Malfoy. Why is there an Draco Malfoy ?" It was, quite audibly, an accusation. Tom filed that away. Uncle Draco ignored the hiss with the ease of a man who had spent decades being hissed at and turned on Tom instead. Tiredness showed in his face, Tom examined, the specific kind he wore only after arguing with his father. “Do you”, Uncle Draco demanded with icy precision, "have the faintest idea how long it took to talk your father out of coming here himself? Three hours. Three hours, an arithmantic projection of cross-dimensional time dilation, and your aunt's entire team on emergency call – because if one hour here is a month at home, or worse, then we cannot afford to lose him into a hole in reality with no anchor and no–" He stopped his rant. Breathed. Recomposed himself into something diplomatic before, slowly, drawing a familiar length of wood from his sleeve – slowly enough not to set off any of the many people still pointing wands at them. "Are you hurt?" "Nothing worth mentioning." Uncle Draco nodded and flicked his wrist; Tom caught his wand out of the air. The relief of his own magic settling back into his hand was profound enough that he almost wanted to cry. He didn't. Obviously. “Your father is leaving instructions…” Instructions. His father did not leave instructions. His father did not delegate. The last time his father had formally handed over authority, even for a day, Tom had been eleven, and the occasion had been a funeral. Whatever numbers Aunt Hermione's projections had spat out, they had been ugly enough to make the most powerful man in their world sit down and write contingencies before coming after his son. The time dilation must be bad. Which, on the other hand, meant his father was coming soon. “…and my task was to secure you until then. Alone ”, Uncle Draco added, with a pointed look at James. “You got to jump through a hole between worlds”, James countered, unrepentant. “To rescue Tom like a damsel in distress. I wasn’t going to miss that for anything by staying at home .” “That”, Uncle Draco replied, shifting his weight – casually, almost incidentally, into the one spot that put him between James and the room should it come to blows, not that James would notice – “is something you should take up with your father. When he inevitably joins us.” James‘ stupid grin wavered. Just a bit. “Nobody is going to find your body”, Tom said. James made a face. “Don’t say creepy shit like that. Dad isn’t going to kill me. Probably. He will at least hear me out, right?” Tom smiled at him. James took one careful half-step behind Uncle Draco. Sensible. Belated, but sensible. “Uncle”, James pleaded, lowering his voice to something that was still, tragically, audible to most of the kitchen, “you must help me. Dad is going to kill me.” “Maybe”, Tom suggested pleasantly, “you should have thought about that before jumping after Uncle Draco.” "Stop being such a smartass." "I did consider dumbass, but it seemed to fit you much better than me." "Fuck you, Tom." Somewhere to Tom's left, a wand drifted down an inch. Not out of trust – Tom didn't flatter himself – but out of the simple human impossibility of menacing three people who were bickering, in the middle of an armed standoff. Killing intent was difficult to sustain in the presence of genuine idiocy. Tom knew this better than most. He lived with James. “Are you two quite finished?” Uncle Draco drawled. He had not once taken his attention off the hook-nosed man in the shadows, which told Tom precisely how much of that byplay had been theatre. The man standing there had his wand in hand, held low. He had not raised it. He had not lowered his guard either. He had done nothing at all except stand very still in the dark and watch – which, in a room where everyone else had spent the last five minutes doing something, made him easily the most interesting thing in it. Then the man in the shadows spoke for the first time. Silky. Cold. “How curious”, he intoned, like he was dissecting something, looking at Uncle Draco with narrow, intent eyes. “Draco Malfoy. You will forgive the observation. You appear to be protecting Potter's sons. Which seems, for this world at least, rather strange.” “Professor Snape.” So that was the Severus Snape . Uncle Draco inclined his head, perfectly courteous, mask up, and stepped – Tom noted – half a pace further in front of James. “Stranger things have happened. I've seen most of them happen personally. Friday afternoons in my kitchen.” Uncle Draco made a point of looking around. “Strange things happing in kitchens seems to be a common theme through worlds.” Yeah, Aunt Hermione did tent to experiment in the kitchen. Uncle Draco turned to Dumbledore, and diplomacy settled over him like a second set of robes. “Albus Dumbledore. I assume the call was yours. Then you should understand what you've done: you have taken the son of the most powerful wizard of our world. My Lord does not respond well to–” Uncle Draco stopped. Half a sentence too late. Uncle Draco – diplomat, survivor, the man his father trusted to speak in the rare cases when speaking mattered – and his tongue slipped. The magic in the room changed. "Your Lord," Dumbledore repeated. Quietly. James winced. Tom caught Uncle Draco's jaw tighten by a fraction of a millimetre – which, for a Malfoy, was the equivalent of screaming into a pillow. Decades of carefully worded treaties and more carefully worded threats, and it was not enough to prevent one slip of the tongue in front of the worst possible audience. Uncle Draco did not make mistakes. That he had made one here told Tom more about how rattled the man was than anything else could. "Thomas and James's father," Uncle Draco allowed, "earned my loyalty a long time ago. In another world, and – evidently – another future than yours." It was, Tom thought, a very good save. Judging by the way Dumbledore's eyes returned to Tom's face – to whatever ghost the old man kept seeing there, Tom was getting genuinely tired of it – it was not good enough. Minutes passed like that. Dumbledore asked measured questions, Draco gave polished non-answers, people muttered, and the ritual circle went on faintly smoking on the floor between them all. Then Uncle Draco checked his watch, and something in his face shifted, and he smiled. Mirthless didn't begin to cover it. The wards of the old house felt it first, somewhere a portrait began to scream and was cut off mid-shriek – it was an old house, a paranoid house, layered wards like sediment and Tom sensed them bristle, assess, and go very quiet, the magical equivalent of a guard dog deciding today was a good day to be a rug. The candles guttered. The pressure arrived ahead of the man himself, that familiar storm-front weight Tom had grown up inside of, and all the people present collectively flinched without knowing what they were flinching at. The kitchen wall did not explode. It simply ceased to be relevant, folding open around a tall figure in black like a curtain being drawn aside. His father stepped through. He did not look like a man who had crossed dimensions. He looked like a man who had crossed a hallway. His eyes swept the room once – cataloguing threats, dismissing all of them – passed over Dumbledore with a flicker of something , snagged for one long second on the boy, his alternate self, by the fireplace, and then landed on them. Tom was powerful; he knew this with the clean, unarrogant certainty of measured fact. He had outgrown Uncle Draco's reach years ago, and James had never been in the race. The family hierarchy ran – in raw magical terms, and everyone involved knew it – Father, then a very long gap, then Tom, then Uncle Draco, and then, after another polite gap, James. Next to his father, Tom was a deep lake standing beside the sea. You didn't compare. You just tried not to drown. “Tom.” His father took in the wand in Tom's hand, the lack of blood, the fact of him being alive and whole. Something behind his father's eyes unclenched by a fraction of a degree, and only someone who had spent a lifetime studying that face would have seen it. Tom had. Tom did. “Father.” Then the green gaze slid sideways, settling on James. “James.” “Dad–" “I remember that you were told to stay." “I was told not to follow–" “You," their father continued quietly, “jumped into an unstable dimensional breach. After your uncle and aunt explicitly told you to stay put.” “Tom was getting to do fun stuff again–" “You jumped into an unstable breach,” his father continued, in the same level voice that had made war councils study their own hands, "with no return vector, no time calibration, and my second-least replaceable wand in your pocket. If the differential had inverted, you would have aged a decade in transit. Draco told you no. Hermione told you no. I told you no. You are apparently unfamiliar with the concept.” Uncle Draco nodded in agreement. James wilted. James was nineteen, half a head taller than their father, and wilted like a first-year caught out after curfew. Tom permitted himself exactly one second of schadenfreude. James, for once in his life, chose survival. "Sorry, sir. Not gonna happen again." It said something about the room's survival instincts that nobody had interrupted. It said something about Dumbledore that he was the one who eventually did. “Harry Potter, I presume?”, the old man asked. His father turned. And Tom watched, with genuine curiosity, as the two most dangerous men in the room took each other's measure across a shabby kitchen. The boy with his father's face wore the expression of someone whose entire understanding of the universe was being rewritten in real time. “Albus Dumbledore”, his father returned. His voice was polite. And Tom, who knew every register of that voice, heard the exact temperature of it and thought: Father knows about the summoning clause. Tom twirled his wand once between his fingers and allowed himself to relax. His father was here. Which meant this was no longer Tom's problem. It was everyone else's.
